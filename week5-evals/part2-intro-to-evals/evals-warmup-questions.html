AI Engineering · Evals warm-up

Ten questions before we start

Ask each question, let the room answer, then open the answer. They read like interview questions on purpose: every one has come up in real AI-engineering interviews, and each answer points back to the step in the code where we saw it happen.

Intro to evals incident-report extractor Week 5 diagram agent
  1. 1

    Your agent replies “Done!” How do you know it actually did the job?

    Week 5 · step 0, 1

    You don't, until you look at the output. What the agent says about its work is not evidence.

    • Check the artifact the user receives (the canvas, the JSON, the file), not the chat message.
    • Start by reading every result of a small fixed set of cases yourself. That gives you a baseline and shows you what to measure.
    • Human 1–5 grading works for ten cases. It doesn't scale, and two people will score the same output differently.
    In the lessonThe first diagram agent said “Done!” for “User → API → Database”. The canvas had three empty boxes and arrows attached to nothing.
  2. 2

    You have three models to choose from. How do you pick one?

    Intro · step 1

    Set the accuracy floor first, then pick the cheapest model that clears it, measured as cost per completed task.

    • The floor comes from the business: here the review team can fix about 1 bad report in 10, so the floor is 90%.
    • Cost per completed task = the whole bill ÷ answers that were fully right. A cheap model that is often wrong isn't cheap.
    • Run each setup more than once. Live numbers vary from run to run, so trust the direction of a change more than the exact digits.
    In the lesson
    modelrightcost per 1,000 right answers
    small79%$0.030 (below the floor)
    medium100%$0.093 (wins, for now)
    large96%$0.487
  3. 3

    When do you grade with code, when with an LLM judge, and when with a person?

    Intro · step 2Week 5 · step 2

    Code where there is one right answer, an LLM judge with a written rubric where there isn't, and a person to set the standard and check the judge.

    • Code scorers are fast, free and give the same result every time: exact match on service and severity, or Week 5's schema, structure and bound_arrows.
    • Word overlap is too crude for free text. The same root cause can be written many ways.
    • The judge reads the input, the answer and the rubric. It doesn't see our expected answer, so a correct answer in other words isn't punished, and it may say “unknown” instead of guessing.
    In the lesson“Pool limit dropped to five after a bad configuration deploy.” The word check said wrong. The judge said right, and it was.
  4. 4

    Why should anyone trust your LLM judge?

    Intro · step 3

    Only after you calibrate it: grade a set of answers by hand, compare, and turn every disagreement into a rubric rule.

    • The judge is a model too. It needs its own eval before it can grade anything else.
    • A disagreement usually isn't the judge being bad. It shows a rule the rubric never wrote down.
    • Cache the judge's grades so grading the same answer twice costs nothing.
    In the lessonThe judge agreed with a person on 23 of 24. The miss was a cause the report called “suspected, not confirmed”. One new rubric line (an unconfirmed cause doesn't count) brought it to 24 of 24.
  5. 5

    The cheap model scores below the bar. Do you switch to a bigger one?

    Intro · steps 4, 5Week 5 · step 3

    Not before you've read the failures. A low score doesn't say whether the model is weak or the prompt is unclear.

    • Group the failures by kind and by field. One pattern usually explains most of them.
    • Fix the prompt or the context, rerun every case on every model, and decide again. Check that nothing that was right became wrong.
    • Better instructions and better context often beat a bigger model.
    In the lesson4 of the small model's 5 failures invented a root cause for a report that stated none. The prompt said “or null” but never said when. Prompt v2 took small from 79% to 100% at $0.048 per 1,000 right answers, the cheapest option. In Week 5, a real system prompt plus the canvas as text took preservation from 0% to 100% with the same model.
  6. 6

    What goes into a golden dataset, and can the dataset itself be wrong?

    Intro · datasetsWeek 5 · steps 1, 4, 7

    A fixed, versioned set of real-looking inputs with what a good answer looks like, covering every kind of case. And yes, it can be wrong.

    • Cover the kinds, not just the happy path: 14 typical, 5 multi-service and 5 open (no stated cause) in the intro; “create” and “modify” requests in Week 5.
    • Freeze it so experiments stay comparable. Add a new version when you need new cases.
    • Inputs must look like production: Week 5's golden_2.json draws its starting diagrams the way the app draws them and adds hard layout cases.
    In the lessonIn Week 5, connectivity dropped because one arrow in the dataset's own starting diagram was never attached. The test data was the bug.
  7. 7

    You ship a change you believe is better, and a score goes down. What now?

    Week 5 · step 4

    Open the cases that dropped and read them before you revert anything. A drop is a reason to look closer.

    • Compare against the previous experiment case by case, not only the average.
    • The cause can be the change, the scorer, or the data.
    • Scorers should change with the design: when the agent got a “look first” tool, tool_choice replaced preservation.
    In the lessonSplitting one big tool into four small ones (query, add, update, remove) made connectivity fall to 85%. The modify-add row showed why: the dataset's arrow, not the agent.
  8. 8

    Your eval says 100%, but users see a broken product. What happened?

    Week 5 · steps 5, 6, 7

    The eval is measuring something other than what users get. When the eval and the product disagree, suspect the eval first.

    • Score the output through the same code path the user's output goes through.
    • Let the agent and the eval share one definition of “correct”, so they can't disagree.
    • A number that doesn't move can still be progress, if it is now true.
    In the lessonThe eval scored the elements the agent sent (containerId, startBinding). Excalidraw reads label, start, end and silently ignored them: 100% in the eval, near 0% on screen from the same run. The fix made the eval run the app's own draw(). Later, one find_overlaps() served both the agent's tool result and the no_overlaps scorer.
  9. 9

    How do you stop a teammate's prompt “cleanup” from breaking production?

    Intro · steps 6, 7

    A small, fast smoke eval runs on every pull request and blocks the merge below a threshold. The full suite runs as a cheap batch.

    • Smoke eval: a few cases that cover every kind of input, a threshold, and exit code 1 so CI fails the check.
    • Full suite: it doesn't need an answer in a minute, so send it through a Batch API at about half the price, within 24 hours.
    • Batch results come back in any order. Match them by custom_id (like r06__small), never by position.
    In the lesson8 cases, pass needs 7 of 8. The current prompt scored 8 of 8. A PR that “simplified” it back to v1 scored 5 of 8 and was blocked. The lines that looked like clutter were the fix.
  10. 10

    Nobody changed the prompt, but quality is slipping. How would you notice, and what do you do?

    Intro · step 8

    That's drift: real inputs change over time. Judge a small random sample of production traffic every night and alert only on a real drop.

    • Sample about 5% of real traffic. The judge grades it, so no expected answers are needed.
    • Alert when a night is more than 2 standard deviations below the last 7 nights, so normal noise doesn't page anyone.
    • Every production failure becomes a new golden case, and the loop starts again.
    In the lessonThe last 7 nights scored 90–100% (alert below 86.7%). On night 14 a new tool wrote “Priority: P2” instead of “Severity: sev2”. The model copied “P2”, which isn't a valid severity, and the score fell to 55%.
Numbers come from Replay mode in the course code: the model replies are scripted, but every grader, cost and decision is computed for real. With live models your numbers will differ from run to run.
